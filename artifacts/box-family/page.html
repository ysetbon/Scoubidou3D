What you are looking at

A box stitch on an m × n face: m ribbons one way crossing n the other, woven, and then every loose end carried straight back across the block and out the far side. That is one round. Every round after it is the same move again — each arm folds at its own free end and runs back along its own line, the same distance past the weave as the round below — so the column rises straight and stays exactly the width it started. Only the last round runs on: those are the loose ends you would tie off.

Why it is a box and not a spiral

Look at one crossing as the column rises: the ribbon on top this round is underneath the next. The over/unders repeat with period two, and that alternation is the whole of what makes this the box stitch. Keep the same ribbon on top every round instead and the identical moves give you the round — spiral — stitch. Nothing else about the two differs.

What m ≠ n costs

Not slack: no arm was turned off its line, so none hangs past the weave the way a lopsided twist's does. What it costs is binding. Take the 1×8 — its single warp passes through all 64 crossings while each of its eight wefts passes through 8, so the whole face hangs off one piece of plastic. Square faces share it evenly.

Where this comes from

The meshes were built by the studio itself and lifted out of it, so the page cannot disagree with the app about the model. That is also what limits how deep the slider goes: every round is another set of meshes, and a ten-round 3×2 is 2 MB on its own. So the 1×1 is baked to ten rounds, the 3×2 to three and the 3×3 to one, and the slider stops where each one does. Nothing stops the model — npm run check:box carries every one of the 128 faces to ten rounds and asserts the fold, the width and that period-two flip at all 114 048 crossings. Every face is a sample in both hands — 8×8 in the studio, ?sample=box-lh-3x2, and the rest — and they are drawn flat, arm by named arm, in the companion sheet Box Stitches.
